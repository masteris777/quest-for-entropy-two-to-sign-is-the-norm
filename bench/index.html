Every cell holds one complex number, not a fraction. The arrow's length is how much of the wave sits in that cell, a real number between 0 and 1, drawn as the bar under it. The angle between the two arrows is the phase, and the phase is what lets two contributions cancel later. Only that angle is real: turn both arrows of a thread together and nothing anyone can measure changes, which is why the phase slider moves one cell against the other. A thread ticking from a fresh start holds the two arrows at a right angle, one cell real and one imaginary, which is why they sit on the axes. Move the phase, or turn the joint table along both axes, and they leave them.

The meeting is the only move that touches both axes: the product of the two tables, then one swap.

4

The settlement

each book lays the four lengths end to end and signs for the line its unitdraw falls in

Four lines, because a line names both parties. It is not P's answer and then M's answer; it is one entry, "P reads this and M reads that", the way a double entry names both sides of a transaction. Two values each gives four of them. Four values each would give sixteen, every combination of the two multiplied, not added; the ones the meeting left empty simply take no room on the ruler. A book's unitdraw is not its dial: the dial is the angle it turns the table by, while the unitdraw is what its own clock shows at that tick, a number between 0 and 1 that nobody chooses.

5

One book, two books, three books

the same table, settled thousands of times

Why the square, and whose lengths make it. A book signs for a line in proportion to that line's length. One book alone is a needle dropped on a ruler: the chance of landing under a length is the length. Two books are two needles on two rulers, and the chance that both land under the same length is that length times itself, an area. The two sides of the square are the same line's length twice, one taken from each book: different books, same line, same length, which is why the square is a square and not a rectangle. Three books would be a volume.

The number of books is the exponent. Each extra book that has to agree multiplies a line's chance by its own length once more, so with k books a line is posted in proportion to its length to the power k. That is the whole ladder, and it says where the three rules hide their difference: when two lines are equally long, every power treats them alike and all three agree, so they only pull apart when one line is longer than the other, and further the more lopsided it gets. Quantum mechanics says the exponent is two. Watch it from the other side by settling with one book and with three in step 4: one book never refuses, because there is nobody to disagree with, and three refuse constantly and hand the long line a bigger win than it has earned.

The third book is a hypothetical third signer, not a thread in this toy.

What to watch

  • A turn never changes a total. The four squared lengths always add to 1.00, whichever axis you turn and however often. Length is what a turn cannot touch, which is why a book can sign for it.
  • The receipt holds. It is set by the meeting and no later turn moves it. A turn cannot entangle a table and cannot un-entangle one; only the settlement spends it.
  • Order never matters. P turns the columns, M turns the rows; they never touch the same pair, so tick P then M, or M then P, and you land on the same table.
  • Every move has an inverse except the last one. Untick is the same turn by minus the dial and lands exactly where you were. Nothing undoes a posted fact.
  • Meet without ticking P. All of P's length sits in one cell, the detector copies a value that was already definite, and the receipt is 0.00. Tick P to 45° first and it is 0.50, the largest it gets: a meeting entangles exactly as much as the particle was undecided.
  • Settle a few times. Each line is posted about as often as its length squared, and no book ever computed a square: each one only compared one length against its own clock.

In physics words

Everything on this page except the last step is unitary evolution, the first of quantum mechanics' two processes. Unitary is from unit: the move is a rotation of the table that leaves every length as it found it, so the squares go on adding to one and a state that was a unit stays a unit. That is the same property you watch in the chip that never moves. In the standard theory the turn is written as a matrix U with U†U = 1, and the Schrödinger equation only ever produces such matrices. The second process is measurement, where the theory abandons the rotation and postulates that the odds are the amplitude squared. That second process is the one this toy replaces: here it is two books signing in proportion to length, and the square comes out rather than going in.

What this is

The toy of Quest for Entropy #17, with its own rules and no library behind them: the turn, the meeting, the receipt and the settlement, run in front of you. Each book's unitdraws are a hash chain, every one the hash of the one before it, so nothing here rolls dice, and pressing the same buttons in the same order always gives the same fact.

Not claimed

That this derives the wave. The turn and the swap are standard quantum arithmetic written as a spreadsheet. What is replaced is the last step: instead of "probability equals amplitude squared", two books sign in proportion to length.
